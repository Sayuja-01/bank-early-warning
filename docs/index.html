A data story in six scenes

The run was visible before it happened.

Silicon Valley Bank, Signature and First Republic failed within eight weeks in 2023. Scroll to see how their warning signs showed up in public filings, using real data from 31 December 2022 for the 158 US banks with more than $10 billion in assets.

2022: money gets expensive

To fight inflation, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates seven times in 2022, from near zero to 4.5%, the fastest climb in decades.

When rates rise, bonds bought earlier lose value. Banks had bought a lot of them.

Hidden losses pile up

Each dot is one large bank, placed by its unrealized losses on held-to-maturity bonds compared with its equity. These losses don’t show up in reported capital.

SVB’s losses equal 98% of its cushion. But it isn’t alone: Charles Schwab’s banks and USAA had losses even larger than their equity, and none of them failed. So losses alone don’t explain a run.

Whose money could run?

Now the same banks, also placed by how much of their deposits were uninsured: money above the $250,000 limit, with every reason to leave at the first rumour.

Schwab and USAA sit at the top left: big losses, but mostly insured depositors with no reason to rush. SVB sits alone in the top right corner, where big losses meet money that can run. Signature sits far right too, with 90% uninsured.

Money that came fast

Dot size now shows deposit growth over two years. All three failures grew far faster than a typical large bank: +40% to +70%, against a typical +14%.

Money that arrives quickly tends to leave quickly.

What the old check saw

The traditional measure is the equity cushion: equity as a share of assets. On it, the three failures look like everyone else, between 7% and 8%, right in the crowd.

The danger was in funding and interest rates, which this check doesn’t measure.

Ranked against their peers

Combining the four warning signs, a screen of these public numbers ranked SVB first of 158. Signature and First Republic ranked 24th and 25th: elevated, among dozens of banks that survived.

A screen narrows where examiners look. Judgment does the rest.

Backtest on 31 December 2022 filings

Silicon Valley Bank ranked first of 158 large US banks on its last report before it failed.

Using only public data from 31 December 2022, ten weeks before SVB collapsed, this screen compared every bank with its size peers on four warning signs. SVB was in the top 10% of the largest banks on all four. The traditional capital check flagged none of the three banks that failed in 2023.

The three 2023 failures, side by side

Percentile rank among the 34 US banks with more than $100 billion in assets. Higher means riskier, except for equity, where lower means a thinner cushion.

SVB stood out on every new indicator. Signature stood out on uninsured deposits; its losses on bonds were ordinary, and its run came from flighty, uninsured money, much of it from crypto firms. First Republic never crossed the top-10% line, but sat in the top quarter on three indicators. Its biggest weakness was low-rate mortgages, which this bond-based screen doesn’t measure.

Hidden losses alone didn’t sink a bank. Charles Schwab’s banks and USAA had losses even larger than their equity, but most of their depositors were insured, so there was no run. SVB was the only large bank where big losses met money that could leave.

Who else scored high in 2022

The ten highest combined risk scores among banks with $10 billion or more, as of 31 December 2022. Most of these banks didn’t fail. A screen like this points to banks that deserve a closer look; it doesn’t predict which ones will fail.

Several survivors share a pattern the numbers can’t see. Hawaii’s banks have long-standing local depositors who rarely move their money, and custody banks such as State Street hold institutional operating cash that behaves very differently from a startup’s payroll account.

    How the screen works

    Built from the quarterly financial reports that every FDIC-insured bank files with regulators, known as Call Reports. The data is public and free from the FFIEC.

    The pipeline

    1. Alteryx reads the raw Call Report schedules for each quarter (the bank directory, balance sheet, securities and deposit details), removes description rows, joins them by bank ID, merges the two reporting forms banks use, and calculates the indicators.
    2. SAS groups banks by size, ranks each bank against its peers on every indicator, flags the top 10%, and combines the ranks into one risk score.
    3. This page shows the results for 31 December 2022 (the backtest) and 30 June 2026 (the latest complete quarter).

    The indicators

    Uninsured deposit share
    Estimated uninsured deposits divided by deposits in US offices. Money above the $250,000 insurance limit is the first to leave when depositors get nervous.
    Hidden bond loss vs. equity
    The gap between what a bank paid for its held-to-maturity bonds and what they’re worth today, divided by its equity. These losses don’t reduce reported capital, but they become real if the bank is forced to sell.
    Securities vs. assets
    Bonds as a share of the whole balance sheet: how exposed the bank is to falling bond prices.
    Two-year deposit growth
    Deposits now compared with two years earlier. Money that arrives very quickly, often from a few large customers, tends to leave just as quickly.
    Equity vs. assets (traditional check)
    The bank’s own cushion against losses. Shown for comparison; it isn’t part of the risk score.

    Peers, flags and the risk score

    Banks are compared only with banks of a similar size: $1–10 billion, $10–100 billion, and over $100 billion in assets. Large banks naturally have more uninsured deposits, so comparing SVB with a small-town bank wouldn’t be fair.

    Each bank gets a percentile rank on each indicator within its peer group and quarter. A bank in the top 10% on a warning sign is flagged, as is a bank in the bottom 10% on equity. The combined risk score is the average of the four warning-sign ranks.

    Limitations

    It measures bonds, not loans
    First Republic’s main rate risk was in low-rate mortgages, which these indicators don’t capture.
    False positives are expected
    Several banks with high 2022 scores didn’t fail. Deposit stickiness, local customer bases and business models such as custody banking aren’t visible in these numbers.
    A definitional quirk for custody banks
    For a few custody banks, estimated uninsured deposits slightly exceed reported US-office deposits, giving a share just above 100%. This likely reflects differences between the two reported items; it doesn’t change the rankings.
    Banks under $1 billion are excluded
    They don’t report the uninsured deposit estimate, so their scores wouldn’t be comparable.
    Small peer group at the top
    Only about 33 banks have more than $100 billion in assets, so each percentile step there is roughly three points.

    This is an analytical portfolio project, not investment or supervisory advice. A high score is not a prediction that a bank will fail.
